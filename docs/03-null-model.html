The (1|schoolid) term is what makes this multilevel. It tells lmer() to estimate a separate intercept for each school, drawn from a distribution whose variance we want to estimate.

ImportantWhat makes schoolid a clustering variable, and what does not

This is the most common early mistake, so it is worth stopping on. Nearly every dataset has categorical variables, and almost none of them belong inside (1 | ... ). Before writing that term, ask three questions.

How many units are there? A random intercept does not estimate a set of group means. It estimates a variance, and it does so from as many observations as there are groups. Estimating a variance from nine numbers is exactly as unreliable as it sounds. Common guidance wants 30 to 50 level-2 units for reasonable precision, with roughly 20 as a floor below which the estimate should not be trusted. We have 160 schools, which is comfortable.

Are these units a sample from a population you want to generalize to? This is the conceptual heart of it. Random effects treat groups as draws from a population of groups, so your conclusions extend to schools you did not observe. These 160 schools are a sample of American high schools. By contrast, a variable like sector has two categories that are not a sample of anything; they are the complete set. There is no larger population of sectors to infer toward.

Do you want group-specific effects, or the variation among groups? If you want to know how each category differs, that is a fixed effect: put it on the right side of the ~ like any other predictor. If you want to know how much groups vary as a class, and to generalize beyond the ones you have, that is a random effect.

So in this dataset, schoolid goes in the random part and sector, female, and minority go in the fixed part. Sex has two categories. Treatment condition has two. Region usually has four. None of those are clustering variables, however naturally the data seem to group by them.

R will not stop you from writing (1 | sector). It will return a variance component and standard errors and flag nothing at all. The number will simply not mean what you think it means.

Two numbers in the Random effects section carry the story. The variance associated with schoolid is the between-school variance, written \tau_{00}. The Residual variance is the within-school, student-level variance, written \sigma^2.

You also get AIC and BIC, two very common information criterion measures used to assess model fit.

NoteWhy REML = FALSE?

lmer() can estimate a model two ways. Restricted maximum likelihood (REML), the default, gives less biased variance components, especially with few level-2 units. Maximum likelihood (ML), which REML = FALSE requests, is required whenever you compare models that differ in their fixed effects, because REML likelihoods are not comparable across different fixed-effect specifications.

This book uses ML throughout so that numbers stay consistent from one page to the next and model comparisons are always valid. With 160 schools, the difference between the two is small.

4.6 Post-estimation task: calculate the ICC

These commands are run after any kind of MLM. They are called post-estimation commands because they require that the model be saved in memory. If you do something else and then try to run them, you will likely get an error.

The intraclass correlation is the proportion of total variance lying between schools:

\text{ICC} = \frac{\tau_{00}}{\tau_{00} + \sigma^2}

Here’s a quick and easy way to get our friend the ICC:

null.ICC <- 8.553/(8.553 + 39.148)
null.ICC
[1] 0.1793044

Roughly 18 percent of the variation in math achievement is between schools. Put another way, two students from the same school are noticeably more similar to each other than two students drawn at random from the full sample. That is the condition that makes ordinary regression standard errors too small.

TipPulling the numbers out rather than typing them

Typing the variance components by hand works and makes the formula visible, which is why it is worth doing once. For your own analyses it is safer to extract them from the model, since a transposed digit produces a plausible-looking wrong answer:

vc <- as.data.frame(VarCorr(model.null))

tau00  <- vc$vcov[vc$grp == "schoolid"]
sigma2 <- vc$vcov[vc$grp == "Residual"]

tau00 / (tau00 + sigma2)
[1] 0.1793109

The performance package will also compute it directly with performance::icc(model.null).

performance::icc(model.null)
# Intraclass Correlation Coefficient

    Adjusted ICC: 0.179
  Unadjusted ICC: 0.179

4.7 Using lmerTest to evaluate random effects

ranova(model.null)
ANOVA-like table for random-effects: Single term deletions

Model:
mathach ~ 1 + (1 | schoolid)
               npar logLik   AIC    LRT Df Pr(>Chisq)    
<none>            3 -23558 47122                         
(1 | schoolid)    2 -24050 48104 983.92  1  < 2.2e-16 ***
---
Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

This is a likelihood ratio test comparing our model to one without the random intercept. A significant result says that letting schools have their own intercepts improves fit enough to justify the extra parameter.

Read it alongside the ICC rather than instead of it. The test tells you whether school-level variance is distinguishable from zero; the ICC tells you how much of it there is. With 7,185 students you have enough power to detect quite small amounts of clustering, so significance alone is weak evidence that multilevel modeling matters substantively.

Noterand() became ranova()

Older handouts call lmerTest::rand(). The function was renamed to ranova() several versions ago. The old name may still work but is no longer the documented one.

model.regular <- lm(mathach ~ NULL, data = hsb.clean)

Residuals:

     Min       1Q   Median       3Q      Max 
-15.5799  -5.4729   0.3831   5.5691  12.2451 

Coefficients:
            Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|)    
(Intercept) 12.74785    0.08115   157.1   <2e-16 ***
---
Signif. codes:  0 '***' 0.001 '**' 0.01 '*' 0.05 '.' 0.1 ' ' 1

Residual standard error: 6.878 on 7184 degrees of freedom

The intercept estimates are similar, which is expected in a null model. What differs is the standard error, and that difference is the entire practical argument for multilevel modeling. Ordinary regression treats 7,185 students as 7,185 independent pieces of information, when students within a school share teachers, curricula, neighborhoods, and funding. The result is standard errors that are too small and p-values that are too optimistic.

One last point students routinely get wrong. The fixed-effect intercept in the null model is not simply the mean of all 7,185 scores. It estimates the average of the school means, weighting schools by how much information each contributes. With balanced data the two are close; with unbalanced data they differ, and the multilevel estimate is usually the one you want.

mean(hsb.clean$mathach, na.rm = TRUE)   # grand mean of all students
[1] 12.74785
fixef(model.null)                       # model-based estimate
(Intercept) 
   12.63707

4.9 What comes next

The null model tells us schools differ. It does not tell us why. The next chapter starts adding predictors, at both the student and school levels, to explain some of the variance we just found.

Random effects treat groups as draws from a population of groups, so your conclusions extend to schools you did not observe. These 160 schools are a sample of American high schools. By contrast, a variable like `sector` has two categories that are not a sample of anything; they are the complete set. There is no larger population of sectors to infer toward.

**Do you want group-specific effects, or the variation among groups?** If you want to know how each category differs, that is a fixed effect: put it on the right side of the `~` like any other predictor. If you want to know how much groups vary as a class, and to generalize beyond the ones you have, that is a random effect.

So in this dataset, `schoolid` goes in the random part and `sector`, `female`, and `minority` go in the fixed part. Sex has two categories. Treatment condition has two. Region usually has four. None of those are clustering variables, however naturally the data seem to group by them.

R will not stop you from writing `(1 | sector)`. It will return a variance component and standard errors and flag nothing at all. The number will simply not mean what you think it means.
:::

Two numbers in the Random effects section carry the story. The variance associated with `schoolid` is the between-school variance, written $\tau_{00}$. The Residual variance is the within-school, student-level variance, written $\sigma^2$.

You also get AIC and BIC, two very common information criterion measures used to assess model fit.

::: {.callout-note}
## Why `REML = FALSE`?
`lmer()` can estimate a model two ways. Restricted maximum likelihood (REML), the default, gives less biased variance components, especially with few level-2 units. Maximum likelihood (ML), which `REML = FALSE` requests, is required whenever you compare models that differ in their *fixed* effects, because REML likelihoods are not comparable across different fixed-effect specifications.

This book uses ML throughout so that numbers stay consistent from one page to the next and model comparisons are always valid. With 160 schools, the difference between the two is small.
:::

## Post-estimation task: calculate the ICC

These commands are run after any kind of MLM. They are called post-estimation commands because they require that the model be saved in memory. If you do something else and then try to run them, you will likely get an error.

The intraclass correlation is the proportion of total variance lying between schools:

$$\text{ICC} = \frac{\tau_{00}}{\tau_{00} + \sigma^2}$$

Here's a quick and easy way to get our friend the ICC:

```{r}
null.ICC <- 8.553/(8.553 + 39.148)
null.ICC
```

Roughly 18 percent of the variation in math achievement is between schools. Put another way, two students from the same school are noticeably more similar to each other than two students drawn at random from the full sample. That is the condition that makes ordinary regression standard errors too small.

::: {.callout-tip}
## Pulling the numbers out rather than typing them
Typing the variance components by hand works and makes the formula visible, which is why it is worth doing once. For your own analyses it is safer to extract them from the model, since a transposed digit produces a plausible-looking wrong answer:

```{r}
vc <- as.data.frame(VarCorr(model.null))

tau00  <- vc$vcov[vc$grp == "schoolid"]
sigma2 <- vc$vcov[vc$grp == "Residual"]

tau00 / (tau00 + sigma2)
```

The `performance` package will also compute it directly with `performance::icc(model.null)`.
```{r}
performance::icc(model.null)
```

:::

## Using `lmerTest` to evaluate random effects

```{r}
ranova(model.null)
```

This is a likelihood ratio test comparing our model to one without the random intercept. A significant result says that letting schools have their own intercepts improves fit enough to justify the extra parameter.

Read it alongside the ICC rather than instead of it. The test tells you whether school-level variance is distinguishable from zero; the ICC tells you how much of it there is. With 7,185 students you have enough power to detect quite small amounts of clustering, so significance alone is weak evidence that multilevel modeling matters substantively.

::: {.callout-note}
## `rand()` became `ranova()`
Older handouts call `lmerTest::rand()`. The function was renamed to `ranova()` several versions ago. The old name may still work but is no longer the documented one.
:::

## Always interesting: compare our MLM to a regular regression

```{r}
model.regular <- lm(mathach ~ NULL, data = hsb.clean)

summary(model.regular)
```

The intercept estimates are similar, which is expected in a null model. What differs is the standard error, and that difference is the entire practical argument for multilevel modeling. Ordinary regression treats 7,185 students as 7,185 independent pieces of information, when students within a school share teachers, curricula, neighborhoods, and funding. The result is standard errors that are too small and p-values that are too optimistic.

One last point students routinely get wrong. The fixed-effect intercept in the null model is not simply the mean of all 7,185 scores. It estimates the average of the *school* means, weighting schools by how much information each contributes. With balanced data the two are close; with unbalanced data they differ, and the multilevel estimate is usually the one you want.

```{r}
mean(hsb.clean$mathach, na.rm = TRUE)   # grand mean of all students
fixef(model.null)                       # model-based estimate
```
