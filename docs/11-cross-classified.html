13  Cross-Classified Linear Mixed Models

Data for this chapter: scotland.dta

This week, we will be using George Leckie’s (2009) scotland dataset, which includes 2,310 students, nested within 17 schools and separately nested within 500+ neighborhoods.

Everything so far has assumed a strict hierarchy: each student belongs to one classroom, each classroom to one school, cleanly nested like boxes inside boxes. Real social structures often refuse to cooperate. Students from one neighborhood attend different schools, and each school draws from many neighborhoods. Schools and neighborhoods are crossed, not nested.

Labelling every variable at the start is a small investment that pays off repeatedly. Six months from now, p7vrq will mean nothing to you, and the label will.

Note one variable in particular: dadocc is each student’s own father’s occupation, scored on the Hope-Goldthorpe scale and centered. It is a student-level variable. That matters below, because the dataset has no school-level predictor of its own, so we will build one by aggregating dadocc up to the school, just as we aggregated free lunch status in Chapter 7. Knowing which level a variable lives at is how you avoid modeling an individual characteristic as though it were a group one, or the reverse.

13.3.2 Data Management Tip of the Week: aggregate a student variable up to the school

scot.clean <- scot.clean %>%
  group_by(schid) %>%
  mutate(dadocc_sch = mean(dadocc)) %>%
  ungroup()

label(scot.clean$dadocc_sch) = "School Mean of Dad's Occupation Score"

n_distinct(scot.clean$dadocc_sch)
[1] 17

dadocc_sch is the average father’s occupation score among the students in each school, a measure of the school’s socioeconomic composition. It has exactly one value per school, 17 in all, which is how you can confirm the aggregation worked. Having both versions in the data lets us ask two different questions later: whether a child’s own family background predicts attainment, and whether the background of the other children in their school does too.

Compare these two numbers with the separate-model ICCs above. Both shrink when the two factors are modeled together, and the reason is the central insight of cross-classified modeling.

A single-factor model has nowhere to put the other source of variation. Fit schools alone, and neighborhood differences get absorbed into the school variance, because students in the same school tend to come from overlapping neighborhoods. The school-only model therefore attributes to schools some variation that actually belongs to where children live. Fitting both at once lets each factor account for its own share.

This has direct consequences for school accountability research. A model that ignores neighborhoods will systematically overstate how much schools matter.

Note also the denominator: all three variance components, since the total variance is now split three ways.

Read this the same way you would read the predicted-value plots elsewhere in this book: the vertical axis is predicted attain, not a coefficient, and each line traces predicted attainment across deprive at one fixed level of dadocc_sch. The values −4, 0, and 4 are a low-, average-, and high-composition school; most of the 17 schools fall between them. If the three lines were parallel, the deprivation-attainment relationship would not depend on school composition, and the buffering hypothesis would be off the table either way.

That is not what happens here, but the pattern is not the buffering story either. At low deprivation, school composition matters: at a deprivation score of −1, close to the least-deprived neighborhoods in the data, a child has a predicted attainment of about 0.8 in a high-composition school and about 0.2 in a low-composition one. (ggpredict() extends the lines to −2 for a round axis, a little beyond the data.) Toward the more deprived neighborhoods that gap closes. The lines meet at a deprivation score of about 2, and from there on the confidence bands overlap almost completely. In other words, a more advantaged school’s benefit is concentrated among children who are already from less-deprived neighborhoods; it does little to offset the disadvantage facing children from the most deprived ones. That is closer to a differential-benefit pattern than a buffering one, and it is worth sitting with rather than assuming the more comfortable story.

Two cautions keep this in proportion. The interaction is only marginally significant, and very few students live in the most deprived neighborhoods (fewer than 2% have deprive above 2), so the right-hand side of the plot is where the model has the least data.

13.6 A caution about 17 schools

With only 17 schools, the school-level variance is estimated from very little information. Variance components need a reasonable number of higher-level units to be estimated precisely, and common rules of thumb want at least 30 to 50. The neighborhood variance, resting on 500-plus units, is on much firmer ground.

This asymmetry should shape how you write up the results. Report both variances, but attach appropriate uncertainty to claims about schools. A school-level variance that appears smaller than the neighborhood variance may reflect a genuine difference, or it may reflect that 17 units cannot pin down a variance with much precision.

13.7 Recognizing this structure elsewhere

Cross-classification is more common than it looks once you start watching for it. Students crossed with teachers across multiple years. Patients crossed with hospitals and with surgeons who operate at several hospitals. Employees crossed with firms and with occupations. Survey respondents crossed with interviewers and with geographic areas.

The diagnostic question is simple. Does every unit in group A belong to exactly one unit in group B? If yes, the structure is nested. If units in A spread across multiple units in B, it is crossed, and modeling it as nested will misattribute variance between the two.

### First, run two separate models with school and neighborhood as the L2 clusters

Fitting each grouping structure alone first is worth doing, because the comparison with the combined model below is where the main lesson of this chapter lives.

### Now, a null additive cross-classified model

```{r}
model.null.crossed <- lmer(attain ~ (1|schid) + (1|neighid), REML = FALSE, data = scot.clean)

summary(model.null.crossed)
```

The syntax is unremarkable, which is part of the point. Two crossed factors are simply two separate random intercept terms joined with `+`.

::: {.callout-warning}
## Do not use the nesting shorthand here
Writing `(1|schid/neighid)` would tell R that neighborhoods are nested within schools, creating a separate random effect for every school-neighborhood combination. That is a different model and, for these data, the wrong one.

The diagnostic question is simple: does every neighborhood send its students to exactly one school? Here the answer is no, which is what makes the structure crossed.
:::

### Calculate neighborhood ICC in crossed model

```{r}
icc.neigh.crossed <- 0.14122/(0.14122 + 0.07545 + 0.79902)
icc.neigh.crossed
```

### Calculate school ICC in crossed model

```{r}
icc.school.crossed <- 0.07545/(0.14122 + 0.07545 + 0.79902)
icc.school.crossed
```

Compare these two numbers with the separate-model ICCs above. Both shrink when the two factors are modeled together, and the reason is the central insight of cross-classified modeling.

A single-factor model has nowhere to put the other source of variation. Fit schools alone, and neighborhood differences get absorbed into the school variance, because students in the same school tend to come from overlapping neighborhoods. The school-only model therefore attributes to schools some variation that actually belongs to where children live. Fitting both at once lets each factor account for its own share.

This has direct consequences for school accountability research. A model that ignores neighborhoods will systematically overstate how much schools matter.

Note also the denominator: all three variance components, since the total variance is now split three ways.

### L1 plus school-level predictor only (`dadocc_sch`)

```{r}
model.2 <- lmer(attain ~ male.fac + dadocc_sch + (1|schid) + (1|neighid), REML = FALSE, data = scot.clean)

summary(model.2)
```

Watch the *school* variance here rather than the neighborhood variance. A school-level predictor should explain between-school differences, so that is where the movement belongs. And it does: the school variance falls from about .074 in `model.1` to about .006, while the neighborhood variance barely moves. School composition accounts for nearly all of the between-school differences in attainment.

### L1 plus neighborhood-level predictors only (`deprive`)

```{r}
model.3 <- lmer(attain ~ male.fac + deprive + (1|schid) + (1|neighid), REML = FALSE, data = scot.clean)

summary(model.3)
```

And now the mirror image: a neighborhood-level predictor should move the neighborhood variance, and it does, falling from about .141 to .061. The school variance drops too, from .074 to .038. That is a reminder that the two factors are crossed rather than independent: schools that draw from more deprived neighborhoods have lower averages partly *because* of those neighborhoods. Fitting these two separately makes it easy to see which component each predictor is accounting for.

On the deprivation coefficient, a word of caution that applies well beyond these data. Families are not randomly assigned to neighborhoods, and families in more deprived areas differ from others in many ways, some measured here and most not. The coefficient describes an association. It does not establish that moving a child to a less deprived neighborhood would raise their attainment.

### L1 plus both school and neighborhood predictors, and the student-level version of `dadocc`

```{r}
model.4 <- lmer(attain ~ male.fac + deprive + dadocc + dadocc_sch + (1|schid) + (1|neighid), REML = FALSE, data = scot.clean)

summary(model.4)
```

With both `dadocc` and `dadocc_sch` in the model, the two coefficients answer different questions. The `dadocc` coefficient compares two children in the same school whose fathers' occupations differ. The `dadocc_sch` coefficient compares two children with the same family background who attend schools with different compositions. Both are positive and significant, so a child's attainment is associated with their own family's background *and*, over and above that, with the background of their schoolmates. This is the same individual-versus-aggregate logic as `TSIG` and `GTSIG` in Chapters 4 and 5.

### Test random effects for `male.fac` at neighborhood level and at school level

```{r}
model.5 <- lmer(attain ~ male.fac + deprive + dadocc + dadocc_sch + (male.fac|schid) + (male.fac|neighid), REML = FALSE, data = scot.clean)

summary(model.5)
```

```{r}
ranova(model.5)
```

This asks whether the gender gap in attainment differs across schools, across neighborhoods, or both. `ranova()` tests each random slope separately, so the two can come out differently. Here neither comes close (*p* = .43 for schools, *p* = .97 for neighborhoods), and both versions have higher AIC than the model without them. The gender gap looks about the same everywhere.

Expect trouble with models like this, and read it as information. Estimating a slope variance across 17 schools is asking a great deal of very little data, so a convergence warning, a singular fit, or a boundary estimate would not be surprising. The neighborhood version rests on far more units and is on firmer ground.

### Finally, test a model with school-neighborhood interaction

```{r}
model.6 <- lmer(attain ~ male.fac + deprive + dadocc + dadocc_sch + deprive:dadocc_sch + (1|schid) + (1|neighid), REML = FALSE, data = scot.clean)

summary(model.6)
```

This interaction is unusual and worth stating carefully. It crosses a *neighborhood*-level variable with a *school*-level variable, asking whether the effect of neighborhood deprivation depends on the socioeconomic composition of the school a child attends. Does attending a more advantaged school buffer the association between a deprived neighborhood and attainment? That is a genuinely interesting policy question, and it is only askable in a cross-classified framework.

The interaction is significant, but only just (*b* = −.025, *p* = .048), and the school half of it rests on 17 schools. Treat it as suggestive.

### Use `ggeffects` to visualize the interaction between `deprive` and `dadocc_sch`

Interpreting a significant cross-level interaction from a coefficient alone is hard, especially one that crosses two different level-2 variables. The `ggeffects` package turns the model into predicted attainment scores you can plot directly.

```{r}
#| label: fig-ggpredict-cross
#| fig-cap: "Predicted educational attainment across neighborhood deprivation, at three levels of school-mean father's occupation."
#| message: false

library(ggeffects)

plot(ggpredict(model.6, terms = c("deprive", "dadocc_sch [-4, 0, 4]")))
```

Read this the same way you would read the predicted-value plots elsewhere in this book: the vertical axis is predicted `attain`, not a coefficient, and each line traces predicted attainment across `deprive` at one fixed level of `dadocc_sch`. The values −4, 0, and 4 are a low-, average-, and high-composition school; most of the 17 schools fall between them. If the three lines were parallel, the deprivation-attainment relationship would not depend on school composition, and the buffering hypothesis would be off the table either way.

That is not what happens here, but the pattern is not the buffering story either. At low deprivation, school composition matters: at a deprivation score of −1, close to the least-deprived neighborhoods in the data, a child has a predicted attainment of about 0.8 in a high-composition school and about 0.2 in a low-composition one. (`ggpredict()` extends the lines to −2 for a round axis, a little beyond the data.) Toward the more deprived neighborhoods that gap closes. The lines meet at a deprivation score of about 2, and from there on the confidence bands overlap almost completely. In other words, a more advantaged school's benefit is concentrated among children who are already from less-deprived neighborhoods; it does little to offset the disadvantage facing children from the most deprived ones. That is closer to a *differential-benefit* pattern than a buffering one, and it is worth sitting with rather than assuming the more comfortable story.

Two cautions keep this in proportion. The interaction is only marginally significant, and very few students live in the most deprived neighborhoods (fewer than 2% have `deprive` above 2), so the right-hand side of the plot is where the model has the least data.

## A caution about 17 schools

With only 17 schools, the school-level variance is estimated from very little information. Variance components need a reasonable number of higher-level units to be estimated precisely, and common rules of thumb want at least 30 to 50. The neighborhood variance, resting on 500-plus units, is on much firmer ground.

This asymmetry should shape how you write up the results. Report both variances, but attach appropriate uncertainty to claims about schools. A school-level variance that appears smaller than the neighborhood variance may reflect a genuine difference, or it may reflect that 17 units cannot pin down a variance with much precision.

## Recognizing this structure elsewhere

Cross-classification is more common than it looks once you start watching for it. Students crossed with teachers across multiple years. Patients crossed with hospitals and with surgeons who operate at several hospitals. Employees crossed with firms and with occupations. Survey respondents crossed with interviewers and with geographic areas.

The diagnostic question is simple. Does every unit in group A belong to exactly one unit in group B? If yes, the structure is nested. If units in A spread across multiple units in B, it is crossed, and modeling it as nested will misattribute variance between the two.
